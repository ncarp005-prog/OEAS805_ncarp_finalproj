# Section 1: Introduction

## 1.1 The Coastal Road Inundation & Pavement Degradation Challenge

Across coastal municipalities worldwide, low-lying road networks designed under stationary climate assumptions are failing prematurely as chronic tidal flooding encroaches onto land once considered dry. Driven by accelerated relative sea-level rise (RSLR), non-storm "sunny-day" high-tide flooding has transformed from a rare nuisance into a frequent stressor on coastal transportation infrastructure (Sweet and Park, 2014; Ezer and Atkinson, 2014; Sweet et al., 2018). In low-elevation coastal zones, elevated sea levels raise local water tables and push tidal waters inland through storm sewers and surface channels, routinely submerging pavement surfaces and saturating road foundations (Knott et al., 2017; Ezer, 2022).

Empirical water-level observations provide unequivocal proof of this ongoing hydrological transformation along the U.S. Mid-Atlantic coast. At the Sewells Point tide gauge in Norfolk, Virginia (NOAA Station 8638610), verified monthly mean water levels record a long-term relative sea-level rise rate of **\\(4.83 \pm 0.21\text{ mm/yr}\\)** over the 1927–2025 observation period (Zervas, 2009; NOAA CO-OPS, 2026a). This trend represents approximately **\\(0.47\text{ m}\\) (\\(1.55\text{ ft}\\))** of vertical sea-level rise relative to the local land surface over the past century (**Figure 2**). As regional sea levels rise, the exceedance probability of high-tide thresholds accelerates non-linearly: water levels exceeding Mean Higher High Water (MHHW) occurred just \\(0.3\%\\) of the time during the 1960s but expanded to \\(6\%\\) by 2021, with minor street flooding hours increasing exponentially in recent decades (Sweet et al., 2017; Ezer, 2022). Under the NOAA (2017) Intermediate-High climate scenario, relative sea level at Sewells Point is projected to reach **\\(0.26\text{ m NAVD88}\\) by 2025**, **\\(0.49\text{ m}\\) by 2040**, **\\(0.90\text{ m}\\) by 2060**, and **\\(1.42\text{ m}\\) by 2080** (Sweet et al., 2017; USACE, 2022; **Figure 2**). Consequently, coastal road networks constructed only \\(0.5\text{–}1.0\text{ m}\\) above present-day MHHW face rapidly escalating flood frequencies that will culminate in regular daily submergence before the end of the century (Ezer, 2022).

While surface water inundation visibly halts traffic flow, the most severe structural damage to road infrastructure occurs out of sight within the pavement subsurface. Flexible asphalt pavements consist of a surface asphalt concrete layer underlain by unbound aggregate base and subgrade soil layers. Under dry conditions, these unbound layers provide essential structural stiffness, quantified by the subgrade resilient modulus (\\(M_r\\)). However, when recurrent tidal flooding and rising coastal groundwater tables submerge the pavement foundation, water fills the subgrade pore spaces and destroys soil suction (Knott et al., 2017; Elseifi et al., 2022). Subgrade saturation triggers a dramatic loss of resilient modulus and structural load-carrying capacity, reducing subgrade strength to a degree equivalent to losing several inches of structural asphalt overlay (Gaspard et al., 2007; Elseifi et al., 2022). When dynamic vehicle traffic loads traverse these moisture-weakened base layers, high tensile strains develop at the bottom of the asphalt layer, accelerating fatigue failure and manifesting on the road surface as interconnected alligator and longitudinal fatigue cracking (Gaspard et al., 2007; Knott et al., 2017; Elseifi et al., 2022).

## 1.2 Research Problem & Study Location

Despite growing awareness that water weakens pavement, quantifying the direct empirical relationship between tidal flood frequency and pavement cracking at the municipal scale remains a major research challenge. Traditional pavement condition surveys rely on coarse satellite imagery or visual windshield inspections that lack the centimeter-level spatial resolution required to detect early-stage fatigue cracks. Furthermore, observational field studies frequently struggle to isolate flood-induced structural degradation from vehicle traffic loading—the primary mechanical driver of pavement wear—or unmeasured local confounding factors such as pavement age and subgrade drainage properties.

To address this challenge, this study investigates the empirical association between chronic tidal flood frequency, vehicle traffic volume, and pavement cracking across **The Hague**, a low-lying tidal inlet corridor of the Elizabeth River situated in the historic Ghent neighborhood of Norfolk, Virginia (**Figure 1**). Bordered by Mowbray Arch, Botetourt Street, and Memorial Place, the study area sits immediately adjacent to tidally influenced open water, with pavement surface elevations residing predominantly between **\\(0.5\text{ m}\\) and \\(1.0\text{ m}\\)** above local MHHW (**Figure 1**). Using an ultra-high-resolution (\\(2\text{ cm}\\)) drone orthomosaic coupled with deep-learning computer vision, a centimeter-scale digital elevation model (DEM), local tide-gauge statistics, and Virginia Department of Transportation (VDOT) traffic counts, this study evaluates whether chronic tidal inundation contributes significantly to elevated pavement crack density across the municipal street network.

## 1.3 Central Research Questions & Scientific Hypotheses

This research evaluates the observed historical association between flood frequency and pavement cracking and models future pavement vulnerability under projected sea-level rise scenarios. The study is structured around two central research questions (\\(\text{RQ}_1\\) and \\(\text{RQ}_2\\)) and three formal scientific hypotheses (\\(\text{H}_{0,1}\\), \\(\text{H}_1\\), and \\(\text{H}_2\\)):

### Research Question 1 (\\(\text{RQ}_1\\): Observed Association)
> *Does chronic tidal flood frequency contribute significantly to increased pavement crack density across a coastal municipal road network after controlling for vehicle traffic volume and road surface classification?*

* **Null Hypothesis (\\(\text{H}_{0,1}\\)):** Controlling for Annual Average Daily Traffic (AADT) volume and road surface classification, pavement crack density is not significantly associated with modeled annual flood day frequency (\\(\beta_1 = 0; p \ge 0.05\\)).
* **Alternative Hypothesis 1 (\\(\text{H}_1\\) — Modeled Flood Frequency):** After controlling for traffic volume and surface classification, pavement cells subjected to higher modeled annual flood day frequencies exhibit significantly higher pavement crack density (\\(\beta_1 > 0; p < 0.05\\)).
* **Alternative Hypothesis 2 (\\(\text{H}_2\\) — Observed Flood Extent):** Controlling for traffic volume and surface classification, pavement cells located within the observed King Tide flood extent display significantly higher crack density than pavement cells outside the flooded extent (\\(p < 0.05\\)).

### Research Question 2 (\\(\text{RQ}_2\\): Projected Escalation)
> *Under projected decadal sea-level rise (2040–2080), how will escalating tidal flood frequency increase pavement crack density across the municipal road network if road surface structures and traffic volumes remain constant?*

To answer these questions, this study integrates \\(2\text{ cm}\\) drone imagery crack classification, \\(0.5\text{ m}\\) LiDAR DEM flood-frequency modeling (Ezer, 2022), spatial econometric controls (Papke and Wooldridge, 1996), and spatial autocorrelation HAC variance estimation (Conley, 1999) to establish a repeatable, data-driven framework for coastal infrastructure risk assessment.

\\[\hat{y}_i = \frac{1}{1 + \exp[-(\beta_0 + \beta_1 \log_{10} F_i + \gamma_{c(i)})]}\\]

Standard errors were estimated using Conley Spatial Heteroskedasticity and Autocorrelation Consistent (HAC) covariance matrices with a 90 m Bartlett kernel cutoff to adjust for residual spatial autocorrelation (**Moran’s \\(I = 0.545\\)**).

## 2.3 Projection Progression & Model Evaluation

To project future pavement cracking under decadal sea-level rise scenarios (2040–2080), the fractional logit model was extended to evaluate the incremental change in expected crack density. Holding vehicle traffic volume and road surface structures constant while updating cell flood frequency to future projection year \\(t \in \{2040, 2060, 2080\}\\), the model yields an updated expected crack density \\(\hat{\mu}_{i,t}\\). To preserve cell-specific baseline spatial residuals resulting from unmeasured local pavement age or drainage variations, projected crack density (\\(\hat{y}_{i,t}\\)) was calculated using a delta-change formulation;

\\[\hat{y}_{i,t} = \min\left(1, \, \max\left(0, \, y_{i,2025} + [\hat{\mu}_{i,t} - \hat{\mu}_{i,2025}]\right)\right)\\]

where \\(y_{i,2025}\\) represents the observed 2025 baseline crack density for cell \\(i\\), and \\([\hat{\mu}_{i,t} - \hat{\mu}_{i,2025}]\\) isolates the model-implied change in expected crack density attributable solely to escalating flood frequency. Predictive performance and generalization capacity were evaluated using 5-fold Spatial Block Cross-Validation across \\(90\text{ m} \times 90\text{ m}\\) contiguous spatial blocks (**Figure S08**). The primary fractional logit model (M2) was benchmarked against four alternative specifications: a traffic-only baseline fractional logit (M0), a linear Ordinary Least Squares regression (M1), a quadratic fractional logit (M3), and a non-parametric Random Forest regressor (M4) using out-of-sample coefficient of determination (\\(R^2\\)), Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), Mean Absolute Error (MAE), and residual Moran’s \\(I\\) metrics.
